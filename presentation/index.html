You have four minutes.

RUN!

That's more or less what our participants were told. Four minutes, as hard as you can: the runners on a treadmill at a fixed speed, the cyclists at whatever pace they thought they could hold.

Meanwhile a mask measured their breathing: how much oxygen goes in, how much comes back out. The difference is VO₂, the oxygen the body is actually using. That's the number this whole talk is about.

How was the data supposed to look?

Moderate

When you start exercising, your oxygen use doesn't jump up instantly. It takes a while to catch up, and until it does you're running on credit. The faster it rises, the smaller the debt. That's why people care about how fast VO₂ rises: it gets faster with training and slower with age and disease.

At moderate intensity it's boring in the best way: up, then flat within two minutes or so.

How was the data supposed to look?

Heavy

Push harder and it no longer flattens nicely. After the quick rise there's a slow creep upwards: more oxygen for the same work. It does level off, just late.

How VO₂ kinetics are usually modeled.

The standard way to get a number out of this is to fit a curve, here two exponentials stacked on top of each other.

Phase I: the first seconds, before blood from the working muscles reaches the lungs. The model skips it on purpose. Phase II: the main rise, the part everyone cares about. Phase III: the slow creep.

The number I'll use later is the mean response time: the delay plus the time constant of phase II. Roughly, the time it takes to get two-thirds of the way up.

Issues

  • Pain to implement
  • Requires arbitrary starting parameters
  • Potential for overfitting

Pain to implement: you decide where phase I ends, whether there even is a phase III, and where to stop fitting.

Arbitrary starting values: the fit needs a first guess, and the guess changes the answer. When we tried 29 different starting points, 27 of 37 runners ended up with a better fit than with our first guess.

Overfitting: seven parameters for about fifty data points, several of which trade off against each other.

A solution? Probably not.

Contrary to any physiological process.

Two straight lines. Nothing in your body does a straight line and then a sharp corner, so as a model of physiology it's simply wrong.

But the corner, where the two lines meet, lands in a remarkably consistent place.

It follows the model.

Corner vs phase II MRT (unfiltered): r = 0.88 runners, 0.91 cyclists trial 1, 0.80 trial 2.

Corner ≈ 1.6 × MRT (median ratio 1.58 / 1.62 / 1.60).

Caveat: the 1.6 holds for typical kinetics (tau ~10–24 s); slower risers give ~1.3–1.5.

Each dot is one person. Along the bottom, the model's answer for how long the main rise takes. Up the side, where the corner sits. They line up: the corner is about 1.6 times the model's number, in runners and cyclists alike.

So the corner carries most of the same information, without fitting a single exponential.

It repeats as well as the model.

27 cyclists, trial 1 vs trial 2. Typical error: corner 3.5 s, MRT 3.3 s — comparable, not better.

ICC: corner 0.73, MRT 0.64 (MRT from fixed 90–150 s windows: 0.74–0.79). CIs overlap.

Both panels span 45 s, so steepness compares directly.

The cyclists, first trial to second. Flat lines mean the same answer twice. The corner wobbles about as much as the model does. Not better, not worse, and that's good enough to be useful.

Smoothing the data barely moves it.

Change = smoothed − unfiltered, per person; both filters (Savitzky-Golay, Butterworth) and all three data sets pooled.

Corner: median shift 0.2–0.5 s (max 3.6 s), within ±2.7 s — smaller than the 3.5 s trial-to-trial difference.

Tau: systematically LONGER after smoothing, +0.6 to +2.2 s on average (p < 0.001 everywhere), up to +10 s.

Smoothing makes the rise look slower to the model; the corner doesn't care.

People usually smooth VO₂ data before fitting, to tame the noise. Smoothing makes the rise look slower to the model, so its number drifts up. The corner doesn't care.

Same story if we use one-second data instead of five-second averages.

A simple, robust, meaningful, and repeatable marker for VO₂ kinetics. Maybe.

Simple: two lines, no starting values, no decisions about phases. Robust: smoothing barely moves it. Meaningful: it follows the established number. Repeatable: the same answer twice, as well as the model does.

Maybe.

As long as...

  • ...the effort is all-out
  • ...VO₂ rises at a typical speed
  • ...it's compared within the same kind of test
  • ...we were neither unlucky nor stupid

All-out: only 4-min maximal (severe/extreme) bouts were tested; moderate and heavy intensity are untested.

Typical speed: corner ≈ 1.6 × MRT holds for tau ~10–24 s; slower risers give ~1.3–1.5 (tau 30 → 1.51, tau 50 → 1.31), so dividing by 1.6 misjudges MRT by ~20%.

Same kind of test: runners and cyclists were different people, protocols and data sources; repeatability was only measured in the 27 cyclists (typical error 3.5 s, comparable to MRT 3.3 s).

Unlucky/stupid: one data set, not yet replicated elsewhere; our own analysis choices (phase II window stopping rule, 5-s bins, 20-s phase I cutoff). The code is public, so anyone can check.

Open: second-trial corners cluster at 43–48 s, cause unknown (possibly the task before TT2; unconfirmed).

So: a cheap, boring, useful number, as long as these hold.

The paper is on its way. Code and data are public on GitHub, so you can check whether we were unlucky or stupid.
